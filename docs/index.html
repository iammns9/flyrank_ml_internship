Refresh Queue: Ranking Content Pages by Refresh Opportunity with an Honest Baseline and a Learned Model

Muhammad Nasir · FlyRank ML Internship Capstone · October 2026

Abstract. (1) Content teams can only refresh a limited number of pages per cycle, so the order of the refresh queue decides how much declining search visibility gets recovered. (2) We rank 30,000 anonymized content pages by refresh opportunity, comparing a transparent rule-based baseline against three learned classifiers on the same client-holdout split. (3) A random forest reaches precision@20 of 0.85 and ROC AUC of 0.75 on held-out clients, clearly beating the baseline (precision@20 of 0.10) and a readable depth-5 decision tree. (4) Error analysis shows the model is most uncertain on thin pages with almost no impressions and on climbing pages the proxy label ignores. (5) The honest reading: this is a prioritization baseline for human review, not a causal claim that refreshing causes recovery — the queue says where to look first.

1. Introduction & Problem Statement

Search visibility decays. Pages that once ranked slip as content goes stale, competitors publish, and intent shifts. Refreshing a page — updating facts, examples, titles, and publish dates — is one of the cheapest levers a content team has, but refresh capacity is finite. The operational question is a ranking question: which pages should we refresh first?

This paper works in the Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane. It builds a scored queue over 30,000 pages, compares a fully transparent rule baseline (built in the earlier ML-07 assignment) against learned models, and reports the honest numbers — including where the models are wrong.

2. Data

We use the starter slice shipped with the internship repo: data/raw/content_refresh_anonymized.csv, 30,000 rows × 44 columns. One row is one pseudonymized content page; metric columns are trailing 90-day aggregates (*_90d), last-30d/prev-30d pairs, and lifetime age/freshness fields. The declining proxy label is trend_direction == 'down' (54.2% of the slice).

Excluded, and why: provider_used and model_used (product/tooling flags, not page quality); content_id and client_id (pseudonymous ids — grouping keys only, never features); anything derived from the label. Public-safe: all ids are hashes; no client names, domains, URLs, titles, or queries exist in this data.

3. Methodology

Features. 18 numeric features (including log impressions/clicks/sessions) plus 8 categoricals one-hot encoded: 52 model columns. The same list the reference pipeline uses.

Baseline. The ML-07 transparent rule: 0.40 × visibility + 0.30 × staleness + 0.20 × position opportunity + 0.10 × thin gap, all percentile ranks, no fitted weights. Built to be explainable and beatable.

Models. Logistic regression, a depth-5 decision tree (min leaf 50), and a random forest (200 trees, depth 10, min leaf 25), all with balanced class weights and seed 42.

Validation. Client-holdout split: 20% of clients (6 of 32) held out entirely — 27,675 train / 2,325 test rows. Same split, same metrics for baseline and models. The queue metric is precision@K (K = 20/50/100); ROC AUC describes the full ranking. Leakage checks: no product flags, no ids, no future windows in features; the label is never an input. The top model features (impression history, position, age, word count) are all plausible — nothing suspiciously perfect.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The label is a proxy, not ground truth. "Declining" does not prove a refresh would have fixed the page.
  • No causal claim. The model finds pages that look like past decliners; it does not prove refreshing causes recovery.
  • One slice, one window. 30,000 anonymized pages, 90-day aggregates. A different season or client mix could shift the numbers.
  • Error analysis. 769 of 2,325 test pages are misclassified at the 0.5 threshold. The model is most uncertain on thin pages with almost no impressions (too little signal to judge) and on climbing pages the proxy label ignores.
  • Decision-support only. The queue suggests where to look first; a human still reviews before spending refresh effort.

6. Ranked Recommendations

Scoring the full slice with the random forest and mapping the top pages to plain-words actions gives the playbook:

1. Work the model queue top-down, not the traffic top-down — the highest-scoring pages are declining pages with real impressions left to recover.
2. For queue pages with CTR < 0.5 and position ≤ 20: rewrite the snippet/title first, then refresh the body.
3. For thin queue pages (word count < 1,200): expand the content before refreshing — thin refreshes recover less.
4. For the rest: full refresh — update facts, examples, and publish date.
5. Keep the depth-5 decision tree as the explainable fallback when a stakeholder asks "why is this page first?"
6. Re-score monthly and re-validate on a fresh client holdout — drift in season or mix will move the numbers.
